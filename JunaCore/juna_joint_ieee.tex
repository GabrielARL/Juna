\title{Differentiable Joint Estimation of the Partial-FFT Response,
Combiner, and Coded Bits}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Gabriel Chua Yu Han}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Underwater acoustic communication channels are time varying and frequency
selective.  Multipath and ambient noise make uncoded symbol decisions
unreliable, so transmissions carry forward error correction (FEC) that
couples every coded carrier in the codeword.  Partial fast Fourier transform
(Partial-FFT) demodulation is an established defense against residual Doppler,
and it keeps short-time views of the symbol interval.  It has been used
ahead of the decoder: pilot-trained combiner weights form pre-decoder soft
symbols, and FEC decoding follows.  However, where
pilots are sparse and residual Doppler strong, separating demodulation from
decoding discards the information the code carries during the combiner fit.
We keep the same Partial-FFT demodulation and begin with a joint maximum a
posteriori (MAP) problem over the Partial-FFT response and coded bits.  This
problem is intractable because the bits are discrete while the response is
continuous.  We therefore relax coded bits to real variables and replace
parity checks with differentiable penalties.  We add the combiner weights
through fits to the pilots and relaxed data symbols.  The resulting regularized
objective contains the response, combiner weights, and relaxed codeword bits;
the views, pilots, and parity constraints regularize one another.  We show
which terms instantiate the relaxed objective and which terms are added for
the combiner.  One receiver computes the combiner weights from the response.
The other permits the weights to move with the response and bits after a fixed
iteration.  The code acts during symbol estimation rather than after it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Underwater acoustic communication channels are time varying and frequency
selective.  Multipath and ambient noise make uncoded symbol decisions
unreliable, so transmissions carry forward error correction (FEC).  One
popular choice is a linear block code, whose codeword satisfies parity
constraints; among these, low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes are the most
widely used.  This paper is not limited to LDPC: any binary linear block code with
parity constraints serves, and we use an LDPC code as the demonstration.
The parity constraints couple every coded carrier in the codeword, and they
are the only constraints in the receiver that do so; pilots constrain their
own carriers and their neighbours.

Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) permits frequency-domain
equalization when the channel is constant over one symbol interval.  Residual
Doppler removes that condition.  After initial resampling a residual Doppler
factor and a carrier frequency offset remain, the channel varies within the
interval, and the frequency-domain operator is no longer diagonal.  Its
off-diagonal entries are intercarrier interference (ICI) coefficients, and a
single full-interval fast Fourier transform (FFT) collapses the time-local
information that would separate them.

Partial-FFT demodulation divides the useful interval into segments and keeps
one view from every segment~\cite{yerramalli}.  It estimates no ICI
coefficient.  It keeps time-local components of the same full-interval
statistic, and complex combiner weights recombine them into one pre-decoder
soft symbol per carrier.  The weights are fitted to pilots, and FEC decoding
follows.  While this receiver is strong and
costs only one small ridge fit per carrier band, it consults the code only
after the soft symbols are formed.

That separation discards information.  Each soft symbol is formed as though
the coded carriers were independent, although the parity constraints tie
them.  Where the pilots are dense enough to track the coupling, little is
lost.  However, where pilots are sparse and residual Doppler is strong, the
weights are fitted from a few nearby pilots.  The constraints that could have
shaped that fit are invoked only after it is complete.

Turbo equalization established the value of exchanging information between
adaptive equalization and channel decoding~\cite{laot,zheng}.  We carry that
exchange one step further for Partial-FFT reception.  Rather than pass
information between two fitted stages, we retain the response of each carrier
as seen in the Partial-FFT views.  This is the Partial-FFT response.  We begin
with a joint maximum a posteriori (MAP) problem for this response and the coded
bits~\cite{juna}.  The received views and parity constraints then regularize
one another while the symbols are still being estimated.  The MAP problem is
intractable because the bits are discrete while the response is continuous,
and a discrete parity check admits no gradient.  We therefore relax each coded
bit to a real variable and replace each parity check by a differentiable
penalty on the relaxed bits.  We then add the combiner weights through fits to
the pilots and relaxed data symbols.  The resulting objective has a gradient
with respect to every quantity.

The two receiver update rules differ in whether the combiner weights are
independent variables.  In the first, the response is updated from the current
bits, and weights shared within each frequency band are computed from that
response.  The gradient moves the relaxed bits while holding the updated
response and weights fixed.  In the second, the response and bits move in every
proposal, while the weights begin to move after a fixed iteration.  A step is
kept only when the criterion does not increase and its pilot term stays within
a fixed tolerance.
We compare the two on equal terms and do not argue that either is the correct
design.  The comparison holds every setting outside these update rules fixed,
so the decoded bit error rates measure the difference between the rules.

We develop this in four steps.  First, we state the joint estimation and
decoding problem in its most general form and relax it.  Second, we write
the Partial-FFT forward model and the pilot-trained combiner used when
demodulation and decoding are separate.  Third, we specialize the objective
for the response and coded bits, add the combiner terms, and give the two
receiver update rules.  The response is updated from the current bits, and the
combiner is either computed from that response or estimated beside it.
Fourth, we report what the comparison measures and the regime in which the
difference should appear.  Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects the
notation.

\section{Joint Estimation and Decoding}

We wish to determine two unknowns from the received signal alone: the
coded bits, and the channel parameters through which they were
received.  This section states that problem in its most general form, before
any receiver structure is chosen, and relaxes it so that descent can solve
it.

Let \(\mathbf b\in\Ftwo^{n_b}\) be the transmitted codeword of a linear
block code with parity-check matrix \(\mathbf P\in\Ftwo^{m\times n_b}\), and
let
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal C=\left\{\mathbf b\in\Ftwo^{n_b}:\mathbf P\mathbf b^{T}=\mathbf 0
  \ \text{over}\ \Ftwo\right\}
  \label{eq:codebook}
\end{equation}
be the codebook, where \(n_b\) is the coded-bit length and \(m\) is the
number of parity constraints.  Every transmission is a member of
\(\mathcal C\), so the code is a property of the transmitted signal itself,
not a processing stage of the receiver.

The modulation map turns the bits into transmitted symbols
\(\mathbf s(\mathbf b)\), and the channel turns the symbols into
the received signal:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf Y=\mathcal A\!\left(\bm\psi,\mathbf s(\mathbf b)\right)+\mathbf n,
  \label{eq:general-forward}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf Y\) collects the received samples the receiver keeps,
\(\mathcal A\) is the forward map, \(\bm\psi\) collects the unknown channel
parameters, and \(\mathbf n\) is noise.  Everything the receiver will ever
know about the bits and the channel is in \(\mathbf Y\), and both unknowns
enter it together.

The joint maximum a posteriori (MAP) formulation asks for both at once:
\begin{equation}
  (\widehat{\mathbf b},\widehat{\bm\psi})
  =
  \arg\max_{\mathbf b\in\mathcal C,\;\bm\psi}
  p(\mathbf Y\mid\mathbf b,\bm\psi)\,p(\bm\psi),
  \label{eq:general-map}
\end{equation}
where \(p(\mathbf Y\mid\mathbf b,\bm\psi)\) is the likelihood under
\eqref{eq:general-forward} and \(p(\bm\psi)\) carries what is known about
the channel before reception.  Known pilots enter the likelihood through the
fixed transmitted symbols in \(\mathbf s(\mathbf b)\).  The two unknowns are
determined together.  Hence the pilots, which constrain the channel
parameters, also improve the decision on the bits; and the parity checks,
which constrain the bits, also improve the channel estimate.

A receiver that performs demodulation and decoding separately does not
solve \eqref{eq:general-map}.  It estimates \(\bm\psi\) from pilots alone,
forms soft symbols with that estimate, and decodes from them.  Write the
soft symbols as
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde{\mathbf X}=g(\mathbf Y),
  \label{eq:soft-symbol-statistic}
\end{equation}
where \(g\) is the map the pilots trained and \(\widetilde{\mathbf X}\) is
what the decoder reads.  Given the pilots, the chain
\(\mathbf b\rightarrow\mathbf Y\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathbf X}\) is Markov,
since \(\widetilde{\mathbf X}\) is computed from \(\mathbf Y\) and nothing
else.  The data-processing inequality then bounds what any decoder can
recover from the soft symbols:
\begin{equation}
  I(\mathbf b;\widetilde{\mathbf X})
  \le
  I(\mathbf b;\mathbf Y),
  \label{eq:dpi}
\end{equation}
where \(I\) denotes mutual information.  Equality holds exactly when
\(\widetilde{\mathbf X}\) is sufficient for \(\mathbf b\), that is, when
\(p(\mathbf b\mid\mathbf Y)=p(\mathbf b\mid\widetilde{\mathbf X})\).  When
the pilots alone specify the channel well, the soft symbols are close to
sufficient and the separation costs little.  A small example shows what the inequality hides.  Let the receiver keep
two samples of two coded symbols,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf y
  =
  X_1\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}
  +\alpha X_2\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}
  +\mathbf n,
  \label{eq:two-sample-example}
\end{equation}
where \(X_1\) is the symbol the soft symbol is for, \(X_2\) is a
neighbouring coded symbol, and \(\alpha\) sets how strongly the neighbour
enters.  The average of the two samples recovers \(X_1\) and cancels the
neighbour.  The difference of the two samples carries \(\alpha X_2\)
alone.  A scalar soft symbol keeps one combination of the two samples and
discards the other.  Whatever the discarded combination said about
\(X_2\) --- a coded symbol, tied to every other coded bit by the parity
constraints --- is lost to the decoder.

The loss runs in the other direction as well.  The parity constraints
restrict which transmitted symbol patterns are possible, so a channel
estimate that uses them cannot get worse and can improve.  A receiver that
fixes the channel estimate before decoding, and then decodes from
\(\widetilde{\mathbf X}\) alone, forgoes both pieces of information.

Two features make \eqref{eq:general-map} intractable.  The codebook doubles
with every message bit, so the search over \(\mathcal C\) cannot be carried
out directly.  The bits are discrete while \(\bm\psi\) is continuous, so no
gradient connects the two searches.

For differentiability we avoid the step and use a tanh approximation.  A
hard decision on a coded bit is a step: the bipolar value jumps from \(-1\)
to \(+1\), and a step has no usable gradient.  Each coded bit therefore
takes a real variable \(z_i\), and its relaxed bipolar value is
\begin{equation}
  \xi_i(\mathbf z)=\tanh z_i,
  \qquad z_i\in\R,
  \label{eq:relaxed-bit}
\end{equation}
where \(z_i\) is the real variable for coded bit \(i\).  The tanh agrees
with the step for large \(\left|z_i\right|\), intermediate values carry the
bit's uncertainty, and the gradient exists everywhere
(Fig.~\ref{fig:step-tanh}).  The relaxed symbols \(\mathbf s(\mathbf z)\)
follow through the same modulation map.

The parity constraints become a penalty on the relaxed bits:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal R_{\mathrm{par}}(\mathbf z)
  =
  \sum_{j=1}^{m}
  \left(1-\prod_{i\in\mathcal N_j}\xi_i(\mathbf z)\right)^{2},
  \label{eq:parity-penalty}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal N_j\) is the set of bits that parity check \(j\)
constrains.  For hard bipolar values the product equals one exactly when the
check is satisfied, so the penalty vanishes exactly on codewords.

The relaxed joint problem is then one objective:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L(\bm\psi,\mathbf z)
  =
  -\log p\!\left(\mathbf Y\mid\mathbf s(\mathbf z),\bm\psi\right)
  -\log p(\bm\psi)
  +\lambda_{\mathrm p}\,\mathcal R_{\mathrm{par}}(\mathbf z),
  \label{eq:general-objective}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_{\mathrm p}\) weighs the parity penalty against the
likelihood.  The objective has a gradient with respect to every variable, so
demodulation and decoding can improve by the same descent.  The objective is
a surrogate, not the MAP problem itself: its minimizer depends on
\(\lambda_{\mathrm p}\), the relaxed bits need not be hard, and validity is
still checked on the hardened bits.

Three things make \eqref{eq:general-objective} concrete: the modulation
that fixes \(\mathbf s\), the demodulation that fixes \(\mathbf Y\), and the
channel parameters \(\bm\psi\).  The next section supplies the
first two for OFDM under residual Doppler, ending with the pilot-trained
combiner whose output the decoder reads.  The section after it names
\(\bm\psi\) as the Partial-FFT response \(\mathbf C\) and specializes the
objective.  It also introduces \(\mathbf W\), the combiner weights that
parameterize the map \(g\).

\section{OFDM Demodulation and Partial-FFT Combining}

The forward map \(\mathcal A\) of \eqref{eq:general-forward} is a general
term, and nothing so far fixes the modulation or the channel.  In our case
the modulation is OFDM over an underwater acoustic channel.  This section is
that special case.  In it, the received signal \(\mathbf Y\) becomes a
collection of view vectors, and the forward map becomes the channel followed
by windowed FFTs.  The channel parameters \(\bm\psi\) are named in the
next section.  We proceed in three parts: OFDM and residual Doppler, the
Partial-FFT views, and the pilot-trained combiner weights.

\subsection{OFDM and Residual Doppler}

Let \(N\) be the number of OFDM samples after cyclic-prefix removal, let
\(\mathbf r\in\C^N\) be the received block, and let
\(\mathbf F\in\C^{N\times N}\) be the unitary FFT matrix.  The active-carrier
set is \(\Kset\).  The transmitted symbol on carrier \(k\) is
\(X_k\), and the transmitted block is \(\mathbf F^H\mathbf X\).  Full-FFT
demodulation takes one number per carrier:
\begin{equation}
  Y_k^{\mathrm{full}}
  =
  \mathbf e_k^{T}\mathbf F\mathbf r,
  \label{eq:full-fft-bin}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf e_k\) selects bin \(k\), the FFT output at carrier
\(k\).  With an adequate cyclic prefix and a channel that does not vary over the
block, cyclic-prefix removal leaves a circulant operator, the FFT
diagonalizes it, and every carrier obeys the one-tap model
\begin{equation}
  Y_k^{\mathrm{full}}
  =
  H_{k,k}X_k+\nu_k,
  \label{eq:one-tap}
\end{equation}
where \(H_{k,k}\) is the channel gain on carrier \(k\) and \(\nu_k\) is
noise.
One equalizer tap per carrier then suffices, and pilots specify it.

We write the channel at the operator level.  Let \(\mathbf G\)
be the time-domain channel operator over one useful block after
cyclic-prefix removal, so that
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf Y^{\mathrm{full}}
  =
  \mathbf F\mathbf G\mathbf F^{H}\mathbf X+\bm\nu
  \triangleq
  \mathbf H_{\mathrm{fd}}\mathbf X+\bm\nu,
  \label{eq:fd-operator}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf H_{\mathrm{fd}}\) collects every coefficient
\(H_{k,\ell}\) and \(\bm\nu\) is the noise vector.  When the channel does
not vary over the block, \(\mathbf G\) is circulant and
\(\mathbf H_{\mathrm{fd}}\) is diagonal.  Residual Doppler makes
\(\mathbf G\) non-circulant and \(\mathbf H_{\mathrm{fd}}\)
non-diagonal (Fig.~\ref{fig:hfd-band}).

Motion time-scales the received waveform.  After initial resampling a
residual Doppler factor \(a\) and a carrier frequency offset \(\theta\)
remain, so carrier \(\ell\) arrives at index \((1+a)\ell+\theta\) rather than
at \(\ell\).  Bin \(k\) multiplies by \(e^{-j2\pi kn/N}\) and averages, so
carrier \(\ell\) enters bin \(k\) with the coefficient
\begin{align}
  H_{k,\ell}
  &=
  \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
  e^{j2\pi\Delta_{k,\ell}n/N},
  \label{eq:ici-coefficient}\\
  \Delta_{k,\ell}
  &=
  (\ell-k)+\theta+a\ell,
  \label{eq:leftover-turns}
\end{align}
where \(\Delta_{k,\ell}\) is the number of turns carrier \(\ell\) makes
relative to bin \(k\) over one block.  When \(a\) and \(\theta\) are zero the
turn count is an integer, the sum closes, and the coefficient vanishes for
every \(\ell\neq k\); this is subcarrier orthogonality.  A nonzero \(a\) or
\(\theta\) makes the turn count fractional, and the residue is the ICI
coefficient.  Collecting the coefficients of all active carriers,
\begin{equation}
  Y_k^{\mathrm{full}}
  =
  H_{k,k}X_k
  +
  \sum_{\ell\in\Kset\setminus\{k\}}
  H_{k,\ell}X_\ell
  +
  \nu_k,
  \label{eq:ici-row}
\end{equation}
where the first term carries the wanted symbol and the sum is the ICI.  The
magnitude of \eqref{eq:ici-coefficient} is a Dirichlet kernel in
\(\Delta_{k,\ell}\), so for small \(a\) and \(\theta\) the coefficients stay
near the diagonal and the operator is banded.

Hence we use the banded approximation shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:hfd-band}:
\begin{equation}
  Y_k^{\mathrm{full}}
  =
  H_{k,k}X_k
  +\sum_{q\in\mathcal B_K(k)\setminus\{k\}}H_{k,q}X_q
  +\nu_k,
  \label{eq:banded}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal B_K(k)=\{q\in\Kset:\left|q-k\right|\le K_{\mathrm{ICI}}\}\)
is the local ICI neighbourhood and \(K_{\mathrm{ICI}}\) its half-bandwidth.
The truncation error is
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_k^{(K)}
  =\sum_{q\notin\mathcal B_K(k)}H_{k,q}X_q,
  \qquad
  \E\left|\epsilon_k^{(K)}\right|^2
  =\sum_{q\notin\mathcal B_K(k)}\left|H_{k,q}\right|^2\E\left|X_q\right|^2,
  \label{eq:truncation}
\end{equation}
for independent zero-mean symbols, where \(\E\) denotes expectation.
Increasing \(K_{\mathrm{ICI}}\) reduces the bias of the model but
increases the number of coefficients a receiver must estimate.

\subsection{Partial-FFT Views}

Partial-FFT demodulation divides the useful interval into \(M\) segments.
Let \(g_m[n]\), \(m=1,\ldots,M\), be deterministic non-overlapping segment
windows, and define
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf S_m
  =
  \operatorname{diag}\!\left(g_m[0],\ldots,g_m[N-1]\right).
  \label{eq:segment}
\end{equation}
Partial-FFT view \(m\) on carrier \(k\) is
\begin{equation}
  Y_{k,m}
  =
  \mathbf e_k^{T}\mathbf F\mathbf S_m\mathbf r,
  \qquad
  \mathbf Y_k
  =
  [Y_{k,1},\ldots,Y_{k,M}]^T\in\C^M,
  \label{eq:view}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf Y_k\) collects the \(M\) views of carrier \(k\).
Equivalently, in sample notation,
\begin{equation}
  Y_{k,m}
  =
  \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
  g_m[n]\,r[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N},
  \label{eq:view-sample}
\end{equation}
where \(r[n]\) is sample \(n\) of the received block.  Each view is the
FFT of one windowed piece of the block, taken at bin \(k\).  When the
windows form a partition of unity, \(\sum_{m=1}^{M}g_m[n]=1\), the views sum
to the full-FFT bin:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{m=1}^{M}Y_{k,m}
  =
  \mathbf e_k^T\mathbf F
  \left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf S_m\right)\mathbf r
  =
  Y_k^{\mathrm{full}}.
  \label{eq:view-identity}
\end{equation}
Partial-FFT demodulation therefore creates no new information; it keeps
temporally localized components of the same full-interval statistic, which a
single full-interval FFT would collapse.

In the notation of \eqref{eq:general-forward}, the received signal
\(\mathbf Y\) is the collection of view vectors
\(\{\mathbf Y_k:k\in\Kset\}\).

\subsection{The Pilot-Trained Combiner}

When demodulation and decoding are separate, the views of every carrier are
combined into one soft symbol before the decoder reads them.  That combiner
is the pilot-trained map \(g\) of \eqref{eq:soft-symbol-statistic} made
concrete.

The views help because residual Doppler is mainly a slow phase drift within
one OFDM symbol.  Partition the useful interval into segment index sets
\(\mathcal T_m\), and let the channel be fixed across the symbol while a
phase \(\phi_m\) evolves from segment to segment:
\begin{equation}
  r[n]
  \approx
  e^{j\phi_m}
  \sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}h_\ell\,x[(n-\ell)\bmod N]+\nu[n],
  \qquad n\in\mathcal T_m,
  \label{eq:piecewise-phase}
\end{equation}
where \(h_\ell\) are the \(L\) channel taps and \(x[n]\) is the
transmitted block.  Each view then obeys
\begin{equation}
  Y_{k,m}
  \approx
  e^{j\phi_m}H_{k,k}X_k + I_{k,m}+\nu_{k,m},
  \label{eq:view-phase-model}
\end{equation}
where \(I_{k,m}\) collects the local ICI the approximation neglects and
\(\nu_{k,m}\) is the noise in view \(m\).  The views of one carrier
differ mainly by the per-segment phases, so one complex weight per view can
realign them.  This poses the question the combiner answers: given the
\(M\) views of carrier \(k\), which linear combination recovers a
reliable pre-decoder soft symbol?

The pre-decoder soft symbol is
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde X_k=\mathbf W_k^{H}\mathbf Y_k,
  \label{eq:combiner}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf W_k\in\C^M\) collects the combiner weights for carrier
\(k\).
The weights rotate and scale the views back toward the transmitted symbol,
and the pilots supply the carriers where that symbol is known.

The pilot set is \(\Pset\), and the data set is
\(\Dset=\Kset\setminus\Pset\).  Let \(\Pset(k)\subseteq\Pset\) be the
nearby pilot set for carrier \(k\).  The pilot-trained ridge criterion is
\begin{equation}
  J_k(\mathbf W_k)
  =
  \sum_{p\in\Pset(k)}
  \left|\mathbf W_k^{H}\mathbf Y_p-X_p\right|^2
  +\delta\left\lVert\mathbf W_k\right\rVert_2^2,
  \label{eq:ridge-criterion}
\end{equation}
where \(\delta>0\) is the ridge parameter.  Each pilot contributes one
constraint built from its \(M\) views, and the ridge keeps the fit stable
when the pilots are few.  With
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf R_k=\sum_{p\in\Pset(k)}\mathbf Y_p\mathbf Y_p^{H}+\delta\mathbf I,
  \qquad
  \mathbf r_k=\sum_{p\in\Pset(k)}\mathbf Y_pX_p^{*},
  \label{eq:pilot-correlations}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf R_k\) is the pilot correlation matrix and
\(\mathbf r_k\) the pilot cross-correlation vector, the minimizer of
\eqref{eq:ridge-criterion} solves the normal equations
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf R_k\widehat{\mathbf W}_k=\mathbf r_k,
  \qquad
  \widehat{\mathbf W}_k=\mathbf R_k^{-1}\mathbf r_k.
  \label{eq:batch-solution}
\end{equation}
One small linear system per carrier recovers the \(M\) weights.

The same criterion admits a recursive update as pilots arrive.  For pilot
regressors \(\mathbf u_t=\mathbf Y_{p_t}\), pilot symbols
\(d_t=X_{p_t}\), and forgetting factor \(\beta\in(0,1]\),
\begin{align}
  \mathbf g_t
  &=
  \frac{\mathbf Q_{t-1}\mathbf u_t}
  {\beta+\mathbf u_t^{H}\mathbf Q_{t-1}\mathbf u_t},
  \label{eq:rls-gain}\\
  e_t
  &=
  d_t-\mathbf W_{k,t-1}^{H}\mathbf u_t,
  \label{eq:rls-error}\\
  \mathbf W_{k,t}
  &=
  \mathbf W_{k,t-1}+\mathbf g_t e_t^{*},
  \label{eq:rls-update}\\
  \mathbf Q_t
  &=
  \beta^{-1}
  \left(\mathbf Q_{t-1}-\mathbf g_t\mathbf u_t^{H}\mathbf Q_{t-1}\right),
  \label{eq:rls-inverse}
\end{align}
where \(\mathbf g_t\) is the gain, \(e_t\) the prediction error, and
\(\mathbf Q_t\) the inverse correlation matrix.  Recursive least squares
is the sequential implementation of \eqref{eq:batch-solution}: it updates
the inverse correlation matrix as pilot rows arrive rather than rebuilding
the batch matrix.

This combiner is fitted from the pilots alone, and its output is the
statistic that \eqref{eq:dpi} bounds: the \(M\) views of each carrier
collapse to one scalar before the parity constraints are used.  When the
nearby pilot set is too small to specify \(M\) weights, the ridge trades
bias for variance but cannot add information.  The next section identifies
the channel parameters \(\bm\psi\) with the Partial-FFT response \(\mathbf C\).
It then extends \eqref{eq:general-objective} with the combiner weights
\(\mathbf W\), so that every coded carrier constrains the fit.

\section{The Joint Demodulation and Decoding Problem}
\label{sec:joint}

We now specialize the channel parameters in \eqref{eq:general-objective}.
In this special case, \(\bm\psi\) is the Partial-FFT response \(\mathbf C\).
The combiner weights \(\mathbf W\) instead parameterize the map \(g\) that
forms the soft symbols.

The modulation map is Gray-labeled quadrature phase-shift keying (QPSK).
Data carrier \(k\) carries coded bits \(i_1(k)\) and \(i_2(k)\), and its
relaxed symbol is
\begin{equation}
  s_k(\mathbf z)
  =
  \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}
  \left[
  \xi_{i_1(k)}(\mathbf z)
  +
  j\,\xi_{i_2(k)}(\mathbf z)
  \right],
  \label{eq:qpsk-relaxed}
\end{equation}
where \(\xi_i\) is the relaxed bipolar bit of \eqref{eq:relaxed-bit}.
Pilot carriers keep their known symbols.  Hard bits give the four QPSK
points, and relaxed bits move the symbol inside the square those points
span.

The Partial-FFT response collects vectors \(\mathbf c_{k,q}\in\C^M\): the
coupling from transmitted carrier \(q\) into the \(M\) views of carrier
\(k\).  With the banded support of \eqref{eq:banded}, the views of carrier
\(k\) obey
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf Y_k
  =
  \sum_{q\in\mathcal B_K(k)}
  \mathbf c_{k,q}\,s_q(\mathbf z)
  +\mathbf n_k,
  \label{eq:view-forward}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf n_k\) is the noise in the views of carrier \(k\).  This is
\eqref{eq:general-forward} made concrete: the forward map is linear in the
response, and the symbols enter through the relaxed bits.  Setting
\(K_{\mathrm{ICI}}=0\) recovers a per-carrier model, and a larger
\(K_{\mathrm{ICI}}\) admits stronger local mixing.

The response is tied to the ICI coefficients of \eqref{eq:ici-row}.  When
the windows form a partition of unity, summing \eqref{eq:view-forward} over
the views and comparing with \eqref{eq:ici-row} matches the coefficients:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{k,q,m}=H_{k,q},
  \qquad
  \mathbf 1^{T}\mathbf c_{k,k}=H_{k,k},
  \label{eq:gain-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where \(c_{k,q,m}\) is component \(m\) of \(\mathbf c_{k,q}\) and
\(\mathbf 1\) is the all-ones vector.  The response decomposes every ICI
coefficient into per-view parts, exactly as the views decompose the
full-FFT bin in \eqref{eq:view-identity}.  In particular the channel gain
\(H_{k,k}\) is the sum of the wanted carrier's response across the views.
Without residual Doppler the parts add in phase.  Residual Doppler spreads
their phases, and a weighted combination can realign what a plain sum would
cancel.

The combiner uses the linear map
\(g_k(\mathbf Y_k)=\mathbf W_k^H\mathbf Y_k\).  Under Gaussian noise and a
Gaussian prior on \(\mathbf C\) with mean zero,
\eqref{eq:general-objective} supplies the signal term, a term that bounds the
response energy, and the parity term below.  The combiner weights do not enter
\eqref{eq:view-forward}, so their terms are added through the map \(g\).
After dropping an additive constant, the complete objective has the
decomposition
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal J(\mathbf C,\mathbf W,\mathbf z)
  =
  \left.\mathcal L(\bm\psi,\mathbf z)\right|_{\bm\psi=\mathbf C}
  +\mathcal R_g(\mathbf W,\mathbf z),
  \label{eq:objective-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal R_g\) contains the fits to pilots and relaxed data symbols.
It also contains the term that bounds combiner energy and the term that smooths
weights across neighbouring carriers.  Writing both parts of
\eqref{eq:objective-decomposition} gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathcal J(\mathbf C,\mathbf W,\mathbf z)
    ={}&\lambda_{\mathrm d}\sum_{k\in\Kset}
        \Big\|\mathbf Y_k-\sum_{q\in\mathcal B_K(k)}
        \mathbf c_{k,q}s_q(\mathbf z)\Big\|_2^2\\
    &+\eta\sum_{p\in\Pset}
      \left|\mathbf W_p^{H}\mathbf Y_p-X_p\right|^2
     +\rho\sum_{k\in\Dset}
      \left|\mathbf W_k^{H}\mathbf Y_k-s_k(\mathbf z)\right|^2\\
    &+\gamma_C\norm{\mathbf C}_F^2
     +\gamma_W\sum_{k\in\Kset}\norm{\mathbf W_k}_2^2\\
    &+\gamma_S\sum_{k\in\Kset}\norm{\mathbf W_{k+1}-\mathbf W_k}_2^2\\
    &+\lambda_{\mathrm p}\,\mathcal R_{\mathrm{par}}(\mathbf z),
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:joint-objective}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf C\) collects every \(\mathbf c_{k,q}\), \(\mathbf W\)
collects every \(\mathbf W_k\), and
\(\lambda_{\mathrm d},\eta,\rho,\gamma_C,\gamma_W,\gamma_S,
\lambda_{\mathrm p}\) weigh the terms.  The signal term comes from the
likelihood in \eqref{eq:general-objective}, and
\(\gamma_C\norm{\mathbf C}_F^2\) comes from the response prior in that
objective.  The parity term in \eqref{eq:general-objective} is carried over
unchanged.  The signal term, response bound, and parity term therefore
instantiate \eqref{eq:general-objective} with
\(\bm\psi=\mathbf C\).  The fits to pilots and relaxed data symbols, the bound
on combiner energy, and the smoothness across neighbouring carriers form
\(\mathcal R_g\).  They are not terms of \eqref{eq:general-objective}.

The objective is not an identifiable physical channel estimator.  Different
triples \((\mathbf C,\mathbf W,\mathbf z)\) can reproduce the same views,
and no term forces the combiner weights to invert the response.  The
response exposes the coupled-carrier structure, and the pilots and parity
constraints select among the triples that fit.

The two receiver update rules use these terms, but they do not both minimize
one fixed objective.  In the first receiver, the combiner weights are not
independent variables.  Their per-carrier form is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf W_k
  =
  \frac{\mathbf c_{k,k}}
  {\norm{\mathbf c_{k,k}}_2^2+\delta},
  \label{eq:derived-weights}
\end{equation}
where \(\delta>0\) plays the same ridge role as in
\eqref{eq:ridge-criterion}.  The executable receiver ties carriers in the
same frequency band and applies \eqref{eq:derived-weights} to the response
shared within each band.  The response is updated from the current bits, and
the weights are then computed from that response.  The bit gradient is
evaluated with the updated response and computed weights fixed.

In the second receiver, the response and bits move in every proposal, and
the weights begin to move after a fixed iteration.  Equation
\eqref{eq:joint-objective} shows the common terms, not the executable
acceptance criterion.  Let \(\overline{\mathcal J}\) denote \(\mathcal J\)
with its signal term replaced by its expected value under the relaxed symbols.
This expected squared error includes their variance.  At decoder checkpoint
\(t\), the executable proposal criterion is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathcal J_{\mathrm{prop}}^{(t)}(\mathbf C,\mathbf W,\mathbf z)
    ={}&\overline{\mathcal J}(\mathbf C,\mathbf W,\mathbf z)\\
    &+\tau_C\norm{\mathbf C-\mathbf C_{\mathrm{ref}}}_F^2\\
    &+\tau_W\norm{\mathbf W-\mathbf W_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(t)}}_F^2\\
    &+\tau_T\sum_s\norm{\mathbf C_{s+1}-\mathbf C_s}_F^2,
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:proposal-objective}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf C_{\mathrm{ref}}\) and \(\mathbf W_{\mathrm{ref}}^{(t)}\) are
the response and weight references, \(\tau_C,\tau_W,\tau_T\) are nonnegative
weights, and \(\mathbf C_s\) is the response in OFDM symbol \(s\).
The weight \(\tau_T=0\) when response smoothness across adjacent OFDM symbols
is not used.  The response reference is set before the proposals and remains
fixed.  The weight reference is computed from
\eqref{eq:derived-weights}, held fixed within a proposal, and refreshed
between decoder checkpoints.  A step is kept only when
\(\mathcal J_{\mathrm{prop}}^{(t)}\) does not increase and its pilot term stays
within a fixed tolerance.  A rejected step is halved and tried again, up to a
fixed number of times.  We compare the two on equal terms: they share every
setting outside these update rules, so their decoded bit error rates measure
the difference between the rules.

The last section states what the comparison measures and the regime in
which the difference between the two receivers should appear.
