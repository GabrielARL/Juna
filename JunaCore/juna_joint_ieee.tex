\title{Differentiable Joint Estimation of the Partial-FFT Response,
Combiner, and Bit Latents}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Gabriel Chua Yu Han}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Underwater acoustic communication channels are time varying and frequency
selective, and multipath and ambient noise make uncoded symbol decisions
unreliable.  Transmissions therefore carry an error correction code---here
a low-density parity-check (LDPC) code---whose parity constraints couple
every coded carrier across the packet.  Residual Doppler adds a further
difficulty: the channel varies within one OFDM symbol interval, so the
frequency-domain operator is no longer diagonal and neighbouring carriers
couple.  A separate receiver meets this in stages: partial-FFT reception
keeps short-time observations of the interval, combiner weights trained on
pilots form one pre-decoder soft symbol per carrier, and LDPC decoding
follows.  Each stage is a small linear problem, and the chain works when
the pilots are dense enough to track the coupling.  However, the
separation of demodulation from decoding discards the information the code
carries: every soft symbol is formed as though the coded carriers were
independent, although the parity constraints tie them, and those
constraints are invoked only after soft-symbol formation is complete.
Where pilots are sparse and residual Doppler strong, the combiner is
therefore fitted from a few nearby pilots, and the code constraints that
could shape the fit arrive too late.  We formulate the receiver as one objective over the partial-FFT
response, the combiner, and the relaxed codeword bits, in which
observations, pilots, and parity constraints regularize one another
throughout inference and every data carrier constrains the response and the
combiner.  The joint maximum a posteriori (MAP) detection problem over
these three quantities is intractable, because the bits are discrete while
the response and the combiner are continuous, and a discrete code
constraint admits no gradient.  We therefore relax each coded bit to a real
variable and replace each parity check by a differentiable penalty on the
relaxed bits; the objective above is the result.  Every term then has a
gradient with respect to every variable, so demodulation and decoding can
improve by the same descent: the observation term pulls the relaxed bits
toward what was received, and the parity penalty pulls them toward a
codeword.  Our receivers differ in which variables that gradient moves.
Retaining only the pilot term needs no gradient at all and recovers the
separate pilot-trained combiner.  Replacing gradient descent by the decoder
posterior---the bits held at the belief-propagation output, the combiner
refitted to the pilots and the decoded data symbols---gives the cheapest
receiver, and its fit is solved exactly.  Letting the gradient move only
the bits, with each band's combiner weights computed from the estimated
response of the wanted carrier, keeps every term of the objective; the
response is solved exactly whenever the bits are held fixed, one small
linear system per band.  Letting the gradient move the response, the
combiner, and the bits together faces the complete cost, and there nothing
is solved exactly: a step is kept only when the objective does not
increase and the pilot error stays within a fixed tolerance.  Hence
completeness and exactness trade against each other: the fewer variables
the gradient moves, the more of the objective is solved in closed form.
The estimated response is not claimed to be the physical intercarrier
interference: different responses can reproduce the same observations.
All the receivers use the same partial-FFT observations as the separate
receiver; they differ in when the code acts---during symbol estimation
rather than after it.
\end{abstract}
